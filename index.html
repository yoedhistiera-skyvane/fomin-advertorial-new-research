My friend Liz texted me at 11:47pm on a Tuesday. The kind of text you don't write unless you've been staring at the ceiling for an hour first.

Six weeks into her new routine (a $40 La Roche-Posay cleanser, a $65 Skinceuticals serum, the prescription retinoid her derm had finally agreed to write), her cystic acne was getting worse. Not the same. Worse. She'd skipped her cousin's wedding the weekend before. Said she "couldn't be in photos like this."

She'd done everything you're supposed to do. Cut dairy. Switched to a silk pillowcase. Thrown out her year-old mascara. Stopped touching her face (sort of). She told me she'd started avoiding the bathroom mirror, just looking at her teeth when she brushed.

"I don't know what else to do," she wrote. "I feel insane."

I knew that text. I'd written some version of it three years ago, to a different friend. Adult acne has its own particular grief: the canceled plans, the shame spiral, the embarrassment of trying so hard at something that's supposed to be easy. It eats at you. It makes you feel like a teenager who can't get her act together, except you're 31 and you have a salary and a savings account.

So I tried to help. Until pretty recently, my answer was the usual: see a different derm, try azelaic acid, ask about spironolactone. But over the last year I've been hearing the same weirdly specific suggestion, from estheticians and dermatology PAs and a couple of microbiologists I follow on TikTok. It sounds too small to matter. Then you look at the research.

The suggestion: look at the towel hanging next to your bathroom sink.

Your $40 cleanser is being wiped off with a bacteria sponge

That phrase isn't mine. It's from a comment under a TikTok video about facial towels, where an esthetician was replying to someone who'd asked the obvious question ("isn't washing it enough?"). I screenshotted it and started digging.

Here's what the science actually says, and I want to walk through it carefully, because there's a real claim underneath what initially sounds like a clickbait scare.

Microbiologist Dr. Charles Gerba at the University of Arizona has spent decades studying household contamination. He's the one most cited when this comes up in journals. In testing across hundreds of homes, his research found that roughly 90% of bathroom towels showed coliform bacteria. Coliform is the bacterial family that includes the kinds you really, really don't want anywhere near broken or irritated skin. This isn't a rare problem in dirty bathrooms. This is the average bathroom towel.

A 2023 study published in Scientific Reports (Nature) examined biofilm formation on household textiles over a six month period. The finding: biofilms (sticky bacterial communities) begin forming on towels within weeks of regular use, even when those towels are washed regularly. The loop structure of cotton terry physically traps and shelters microbes in a way other fabrics don't.

If you've been pressing your face into the same towel for the last month and a half, you've been pressing it into a thriving microbial environment. And then you've been doing it again the next morning.

"But I wash mine every week"

This is the first thing everyone says when they hear the bacteria number. It's also the first thing the research dismantles.

Standard residential wash cycles, especially in modern energy efficient machines that don't reach high temperatures, leave behind a measurable percentage of viable bacteria. Detergent isn't a disinfectant. Microbiologists have repeatedly shown that mature biofilms cling to towel fibers and survive wash cycles that would clean other fabrics fully.

There's a second layer to this most people miss. Fabric softener and dryer sheets, the products that make your towel feel "extra clean," coat fibers in a thin waxy film. Many of the compounds used (quaternary ammonium compounds, synthetic fragrances) have been documented in peer reviewed dermatology research as triggers for contact dermatitis, irritation, and acne flare ups in reactive skin.

So your "clean" towel isn't sterile. And the chemicals making it feel clean are themselves a documented skin irritant. The math doesn't work in your favor.

What this means for your skincare routine

Every product you apply is being wiped off against a bacterial film, with a fabric coated in irritant residue, dragged across your skin twice a day. For most people with normal skin, this is annoying but not catastrophic. For anyone with acne-prone, sensitive, eczema-prone, post-procedure, or rosacea-prone skin (which is most of the people who actually buy expensive skincare), the towel may be undoing more than it's helping.

If you've been spending real money on products that aren't working, this is also worth saying out loud: it probably isn't because you're doing skincare wrong. You may have been doing the routine perfectly while wiping it off with the variable nobody told you to look at.
